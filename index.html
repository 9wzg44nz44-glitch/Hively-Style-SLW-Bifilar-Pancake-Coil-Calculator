Near-Field vs Far-Field Boundaries

Because you intend detection at very long range (hundreds of km), it is useful to know where the classical near- and far-field regions end. These boundaries are calculated from the maximum dimension D of the radiating/receiving structure and the wavelength.

Reactive near-field ≈ r < 0.62 √(D³ / λ)
Radiating near-field (Fresnel) ≈ 0.62 √(D³ / λ) < r < 2 D² / λ
Far-field (Fraunhofer) begins at r > 2 D² / λ
For electrically-small antennas (D ≪ λ) a practical rule-of-thumb is r ≈ λ / (2π) ≈ 0.16 λ

3. Bifilar Pancake Coil Dimensions

Should the outer diameter be a full or partial wavelength?

For a pancake coil facing a spherically spreading wavefront from a small stub, a partial wavelength (typically 0.1 λ to 0.3 λ outer diameter) is preferred. A full-wavelength diameter becomes large, moves the structure into the far-field regime for TEM components, and is less matched to the curvature of the near-zone longitudinal gradient. Because the bifilar is designed for B-field cancellation and gradient-driven (longitudinal) response, electrically-small to moderately-small sizes work well and keep the coil practical.

4. How to Wind the Bifilar Pancake & Assign Polarity

Winding method (flat spiral / pancake)

  1. Take two equal lengths of the chosen magnet wire (call them Wire A and Wire B). Optionally lightly twist them together every few cm for handling, but keep them parallel and side-by-side for the winding.
  2. Start at the center (inner radius). Secure both free ends (these will be the two inner leads).
  3. Wind outward in a flat spiral, keeping the two wires adjacent (side-by-side) so that each “turn” consists of A then B (or B then A) alternating radially. This produces the alternating-conductor geometry described by Hively: adjacent turns carry opposite current when properly connected.
  4. Continue until the desired outer diameter is reached. Leave two outer leads.

Polarity / connection for opposing currents (B-field cancellation)

To obtain counter-going currents in adjacent turns:

  • Series opposing connection (recommended for receive): Connect the outer end of Wire A to the inner end of Wire B (or vice-versa). The remaining two free ends (inner of A and outer of B) become the differential output pair.
  • When a longitudinal E-field drives charge, current flows out one lead and returns on the other, so neighboring turns have opposite current direction → magnetic fields cancel.
  • Label the two free ends clearly (e.g., “+ / −” or “A-inner / B-outer”). The coil itself has essentially zero net inductance and low capacitance between the opposed turns.
Do not simply parallel the two wires; that would reinforce the magnetic field. The series-opposing (or “non-inductive bifilar”) connection is essential for the SLW gradient response.

5. Electrical Connection to Receiver / Analyzer

The bifilar coil presents a balanced, low-inductance, moderately high-impedance differential port.

  • Direct connection (strong signals): Use a short balanced twisted-pair or twin-lead from the coil leads to a differential input, or to a 1:1 current balun / RF transformer, then into a 50 Ω coaxial cable to a spectrum analyzer (SA), vector network analyzer (VNA), or receiver.
  • Weak signals: Insert a low-noise amplifier (LNA) or differential LNA as close as practical to the coil. Many experimenters use a wideband LNA (e.g., 0.1–6 GHz or higher) with good noise figure, followed by the SA/VNA/SDR.
  • VNA: For S-parameter characterization of the receive coil itself, connect via balun to Port 1 or use a differential probe if available. Measure transmission relative to a reference path or the known stub transmitter.
  • Spectrum analyzer / SDR / radio receiver: After any LNA and balun, feed the 50 Ω coax into the instrument. For decoding, use an SDR (software-defined radio) tuned to the carrier; for pure detection of the SLW presence, look for the expected frequency component that penetrates Faraday cages or shows anomalous attenuation behavior.
  • Keep all interconnects as short as possible and well shielded. Ground the outer conductor of the coax at a single point near the analyzer to avoid loops.
Because the ideal bifilar has near-zero inductance, classical matching networks based on inductive reactance are less relevant; the design relies on the gradient-driven current and the cancellation itself. Empirical tuning of lead length and a simple series capacitor or transformer may still improve coupling.
